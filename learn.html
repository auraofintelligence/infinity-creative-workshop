01 / Start small

Your first connected film

A short excerpt can teach you how the tools fit together before you build a whole production system.

  1. Choose a useful fragment

    Pick about 30 seconds of your own song, narration or footage. Describe the feeling or idea you want someone to leave with. Keep the source untouched.

  2. Try three treatments

    Sketch an own-footage edit, a typographic version and a spatial scene. Ask what each might add, what it would cost and what you can reuse.

  3. Make one style frame

    Use existing art or create a concept. Check it at phone size before building more. A good-looking concept is still a direction to test.

  4. Build a timing plan

    Mark the words, musical phrases or actions that matter. Choose a frame rate and keep source time separate from edited time.

  5. Assemble and observe

    Bring a scene and a graphic into a disposable Resolve project. Listen to the joins, read the text and compare the edit with your intention.

  6. Keep what you learned

    Save the editable project, sources, export and a short note on what changed. Let that evidence guide the next version.

Something to take forward

A short draft, a reusable project packet and one clear next experiment.

02 / Creative review

A council that helps you choose

The fictional Queens offer different perspectives. Their conversation can help expose assumptions while you retain the final creative decision.

  1. Bring a decision

    Write a specific question with two or three options, your constraints and what a useful result would look like.

  2. Choose a small panel

    Try Dream for story, Spark for alternatives, Forge for making, Prism for accessibility and Shield for permission questions. Adapt the panel to the subject.

  3. Keep opening views separate

    Ask each adviser for an initial position before showing the other views. Shared model training can still produce shared blind spots.

  4. Explore the disagreement

    Compare the strongest reasons, the missing information and what might change each view. Consensus is one possible result, not the aim of every session.

  5. Record your choice

    Keep dissent and assumptions visible. Approve a direction yourself, or choose an experiment that could help settle the question.

Something to take forward

A review brief with options, a proposed panel and questions worth asking.

03 / Music and motion

Let the song lead

Rhythm, lyric meaning and atmosphere can suggest very different visual approaches. A gentle passage may benefit from space; a chorus may invite more movement.

  1. Listen before decorating

    Mark changes in arrangement, vocal character, lyrical meaning and energy. A measured beat grid helps with timing, while your ear helps decide where a cut belongs.

  2. Choose a visual grammar

    Compare performance footage, lyric typography, original illustration and a persistent 3D world. Combining fewer clear ideas may be more useful than adding every available effect.

  3. Protect the source performance

    Keep deliberate pauses, complete words and musical continuity. Speech silence-cutting presets may need different settings for a song.

  4. Make a short visual loop

    Test one motif, camera path or typography sequence against the actual music. Reuse the scene if it supports the larger piece.

  5. Reframe for the second format

    A vertical cut may need a new crop, text size and timing map. Check the face, lyric and focal point on a phone.

Something to take forward

A musical timing map and a tested visual direction.

04 / Educational film

Make an idea easier to grasp

Start with one thing the learner could explain or do afterwards. Build around a worked example rather than a promise that animation alone creates understanding.

  1. Name the learning purpose

    Choose a learner and one manageable question. Note what they may already know and what language needs explaining.

  2. Show a worked example

    Make the reasoning visible. Connect each graphic to a sentence or step the learner can follow.

  3. Include a useful contrast

    Show a counterexample, common misunderstanding or a condition where the idea changes. Keep speculation distinct from established evidence.

  4. Make sources travel with claims

    Record the specific source for factual statements. A fictional adviser or generated illustration can help tell the story, but does not establish the fact.

  5. Leave space to think

    Try a pause, question or small task. Ask a human learner to explain the idea back and use the result to revise the film.

Something to take forward

A clear lesson outline, evidence notes and a learner question.

05 / Blender

Build a world you can revisit

Blender can help create consistent places, objects and camera moves. A modest reusable scene may be a more practical beginning than a fully animated cast.

  1. Choose flat, layered or fully 3D

    Flat art may be enough for a conversation. Layered artwork can add depth. Full 3D offers more camera freedom and brings modelling, lighting and render work.

  2. Create one small scene

    Try an infinity motif on a tabletop, with one camera and one light arrangement. Add an existing portrait as a visibly flat panel if it helps test the council set.

  3. Record the scene settings

    Keep the scene file, frame range, resolution, colour settings and output path together. Use a representative frame to compare appearance in Resolve.

  4. Render a small range

    A sequence of numbered images can make interrupted work easier to resume. Measure your machine on the scene you actually intend to use.

  5. Bring it into the edit

    Check the first and last frames, duration, crop and colour. Keep the editable Blender scene as well as the rendered media.

Something to take forward

A reusable scene and a tested handoff into the edit.

06 / DaVinci Resolve

Complete a repeatable edit

The preparation project already describes the intended editing system. This path helps turn each planned action into a small, observable test on the editing computer.

  1. Check the available controls

    Record your Resolve version and edition, active project and whichever scripting or Astra controls are available. Documentation and installed access may differ.

  2. Use a disposable project

    Try creating bins, importing copied media and making a timeline. Choose settings before assembly and check the actual result.

  3. Place clips deliberately

    Test a small number of frame-based clip ranges. Add markers for lyric sections, learning beats or review notes.

  4. Save and reopen

    Export a project backup, reopen the test and check for missing media. A second run should have an explicit revision or update policy.

  5. Render and review

    Inspect the delivered file, including its sound, rather than relying only on a successful command. Keep notes that distinguish a technical result from creative approval.

Something to take forward

A completed test record and a clearer next implementation step.
